Assortment architecture decides which products earn shelf space, and for regional grocers and food retailers with 10 to 500 employees it decides whether SKU pruning protects a thin operating margin or SKU bloat erodes it. This post covers what industry research says about grocery margins, how the Variety Trap forms, the Discipline of Subtraction, and a one-category test you can run this week.

## Why SKU bloat squeezes regional grocery margins

The economic model of regional grocery retail is under structural compression. For decades, supermarket executives operated under one assumption: shelf variety drives customer loyalty. Store footprints expanded, center-store aisles lengthened, and category managers added thousands of incremental product variations. Every consumer packaged goods brand promised that a new flavor, package size, or seasonal formulation would capture incremental market share.

Industry research points to the limits of that strategy. McKinsey's State of Grocery North America 2026 report, as reported by The Shelby Report, shows U.S. grocery sales grew 1.2 percent in 2025 on price increases of 2.2 percent, while unit volume declined 1.0 percent. Sales grew in dollars while shoppers bought fewer units. McKinsey also found the grocery sector delivered a 12.2 percent weighted average total shareholder return in 2025, below the S&P 500's 18.3 percent. And McKinsey found that 71 percent of consumers prefer lower, more consistent everyday pricing over frequent promotions with higher base prices, as reported by Talk Business & Politics. Lower everyday prices have to be funded by a lower cost to serve, and SKU complexity is part of that cost. Shoppers also have discount, club, and specialty formats to choose from, and a regional grocer competes with all of them for the same basket.

The cost of unmanaged product expansion shows up in three places. Every added SKU takes a pallet position in the distribution center, an order line at the store, and a facing on the shelf. When a new item duplicates something the store already sells, it splits existing demand across more items and slows every one of them down. Slow items tie up working capital and add receiving and restocking labor, and they crowd out the fast sellers that bring shoppers into the store. Removing duplicate, slow-moving items gives core high-velocity products the space and the stock they need.

Bain & Company shows what stronger regional grocers look like. In a 2020 article, Bain used its Advocacy in Retail study, which surveyed more than 14,000 US grocery shoppers with ROIRocket, to identify what it takes to be a regional gem in grocery. Bain describes regional gems as grocers with strong shopper advocacy and market share gains. It says tight cost control often enables them to keep their operating margin in the 4 percent to 6 percent range. It also says private label accounts for 30 percent or more of their sales. Both are assortment decisions. Every SKU carries handling cost, store labor, and working capital, and the shelf is where those costs are set.

The strategic signal is clear. In a market where unit volume is contracting, trying to be everything to everyone costs margin. Supermarket operating margins are won or lost at the shelf edge. Regional grocers that fail to prune category bloat pay for it in inventory carrying costs, store replenishment labor, and chronic supply chain friction.

## How the Variety Trap hits West Michigan grocers

This challenge is acute across West Michigan. The market covering Grand Rapids, Holland, Muskegon, Zeeland, Wyoming, and Kalamazoo is a fiercely contested grocery corridor, and it is home to the headquarters of major grocery companies.

Meijer, headquartered in Walker, Michigan, runs supercenters that sell groceries alongside general merchandise. In Byron Center, SpartanNash, acquired by C&S Wholesale Grocers in September 2025, supplies independent grocers and operates retail banners including Family Fare, D&W Fresh Market, Forest Hills Foods, and VG's. National hard discounters like Aldi, warehouse clubs like Costco and Sam's Club, and specialty grocers like Trader Joe's compete for the same households.

For regional supermarket operators and multi-store independents with 10 to 500 employees, the position is hard. Unlike national discount giants, they cannot win a pure price war on commodity dry goods. Unlike specialty gourmet boutiques, they must keep full-basket credibility for local families.

Caught between these forces, West Michigan grocery leadership teams fall into the Variety Trap. To defend market share against Meijer and Walmart, category managers surrender shelf space to vendor-driven product proliferation. In an illustrative example, a single aisle in a 55,000-square-foot store displays forty-two variations of olive oil, eighteen package sizes of the same cereal brand, and dozens of slow-turning national brand line extensions. These numbers are an illustration, not client data and not a statistic.

The consequence is operational drag throughout the enterprise. In distribution centers, slow-turning items tie up working capital and pallet positions, forcing warehouse crews into multi-touch picking routines. In stores, inventory velocity collapses. In the same illustration, a store carrying 45,000 SKUs sees its clerks lose a large share of their shifts to backroom overstock, changing paper shelf tags, and rotating dated inventory.

Because shelf space is fragmented across too many secondary items, the store's top-selling SKUs, the essential milk, eggs, bread, and core national brands, are allotted insufficient facings. High-velocity items sell out during evening peak hours, creating visible out-of-stocks that alienate shoppers and depress basket totals. Meanwhile, slow-moving specialty items sit on bottom shelves until they expire, driving perishable shrink and inventory write-downs.

FMI reports food retail profit margins of 2.1 percent in 2025. At that margin, uninspected SKU bloat threatens the business. Regional grocery owners and executives cannot afford to subsidize vendor line extensions that dilute customer clarity and drain store labor. Corporate strategy must restore disciplined assortment architecture and protect supermarket operating margins.

## The Discipline of Subtraction in the supermarket

This retail transformation is governed by one of Dipriva's foundational philosophies: Discipline of Subtraction.

The prevailing corporate instinct in modern business is additive. When revenue stalls, leadership adds new initiatives. When customer traffic slows, merchandisers add new products. When operations stumble, management adds new reporting tiers. Business culture celebrates addition because adding feels like proactive progress, expansion, and commercial ambition.

In contrast, the Discipline of Subtraction recognizes that complexity is the primary destroyer of operational efficiency and profit margins. Subtraction is not an act of retreat; it is an act of strategic concentration. It is the deliberate, rigorous elimination of low-margin volume, unproductive assets, and operational noise in order to focus capital, talent, and physical infrastructure on the essential core that generates enterprise value.

In supermarket operations, the physics of retail shelf space make the Discipline of Subtraction imperative. A physical grocery store has finite boundaries: finite linear shelf feet, finite backroom storage cubic footage, and finite employee labor hours. Every slow-turning, low-margin SKU placed on a shelf is not a harmless passive choice; it displaces a facing of a high-velocity, high-margin item. It consumes working capital, requires stocking labor, adds scanning friction at checkout, and confuses customer decision-making.

Applying the Discipline of Subtraction to grocery requires confronting the Myth of Assortment Completeness. Merchandisers argue that a supermarket must carry, for example, five brands of canned water chestnuts or nine package variations of yellow mustard because a customer may walk out if an exact niche preference is missing. These numbers are an illustration, not data.

The argument ignores what an overcrowded shelf does to the shopper and to the store. Dozens of near-identical choices make the decision harder for the shopper and the shelf harder to keep in stock. A category curated down to a dominant national brand, a premium local alternative, and a high-margin private label offering is easier to shop and easier to run. Dipriva's view is that the myth costs more than the gap it protects against.

The Discipline of Subtraction requires executive leadership to enforce three non-negotiable operational boundaries:

1. Economic Justification: Every SKU must justify its physical footprint through measurable gross margin return on inventory investment, factoring in handling labor and space carry costs.
2. Direct Cannibalization Elimination: Identical product attributes across competing brands must be consolidated into a clear good-better-best architecture.
3. Radical Simplification: Shelf space must be allocated in direct proportion to real consumer velocity, not manufacturer promotional lobbying.

When an executive team masters the Discipline of Subtraction, it stops mistaking activity for progress. Removing unproductive SKUs frees warehouse space and shelf facings, reduces replenishment work, keeps vital staples in stock, and releases working capital.

## Run a bottom-SKU quarantine in one category

Run a bottom-SKU quarantine in your single highest-shrink center-store category this week. You do not need assortment optimization software, syndicated data contracts, or an offsite to run this test. The percentages below are working thresholds that you set, not industry statistics or client results. Adjust them to your category.

Select one center-store category that suffers from chronic inventory bloat, high out-of-stocks on top items, or frequent shrink write-downs: breakfast cereals, snack bars, salad dressings, or canned condiments. Pull the trailing twelve-month unit sales, gross margin dollars, and inventory turn rates for every active SKU in that category in three representative stores.

Rank every SKU by total gross margin dollar contribution. Identify the bottom 20 percent of SKUs, the long tail of items that generate negligible cash flow but occupy permanent shelf facings.

Then run a 30-day operational quarantine:

1. Freeze all automated reorders for the bottom 20 percent of SKUs.
2. Consolidate remaining inventory of those items into a single clearance endcap or discount bin to liquidate the stock.
3. Reallocate the freed shelf space to your top 10 percent of high-velocity revenue drivers in that category, doubling their shelf facings.
4. Set a zero-out-of-stock mandate on those high-velocity items, with backroom safety stock aligned to peak weekend demand.

Track total category revenue, gross margin dollars, out-of-stocks on core items, and store stocking labor hours over the 30 days. Your own numbers decide the question. If revenue holds, core items stay in stock, and replenishment hours fall, you have your own evidence that your business does not need catalog sprawl to satisfy customers, and that category subtraction expands operating profit. If they do not, you know which part of the category to restore, at a cost of 30 days and one category.

## Why shelf space is infrastructure, not rented real estate

The common mistake in assortment decisions is treating shelf space as real estate to rent to packaged goods manufacturers rather than infrastructure to optimize for customer throughput. Category managers routinely accept manufacturer promotional funding, advertising co-ops, and slotting allowances to feature slow-moving product variations, believing they are securing free income for the enterprise. That is financial myopia. A slotting check pays for the facing. It does not pay for the ordering, receiving, warehousing, stocking, and eventual markdown of the product that sits behind it.

A supermarket is a high-velocity fulfillment pipeline, not a warehouse showroom for vendor experiments. When your corporate strategy lets consumer packaged goods brands dictate shelf variety, you outsource your operating cost structure to entities whose financial incentives differ from yours. The brand manufacturer wants maximum shelf facings to block competitors; the supermarket needs maximum inventory turnover per square foot to survive. Executives who want to defend margin break this pattern by reclaiming control over assortment architecture, removing vendor-driven complexity, and building a lean product catalog that maximizes return on invested capital.

## Four moves to defend grocery operating margins

Owners, executives, and category leaders at regional grocers and food retailers with 10 to 500 employees can remove assortment friction and defend operating margins by making four strategic moves:

1. Mandate a One-In, Two-Out policy for new center-store SKUs: Halt the unchecked accumulation of product by setting a catalog gatekeeping rule. Before a category manager adds a single new manufacturer SKU to store planograms, they must identify and permanently delist two existing SKUs in the same subcategory. The two delisted items must show lower historical sales velocity and gross margin contribution than the category average. This rule forces category managers to weigh real trade-offs, prevents catalog creep, and keeps clearing dead inventory off store shelves.

2. Audit total cost to serve across all direct-store-delivery (DSD) vendors: Review every DSD vendor serving your stores: commercial bread, salty snacks, carbonated beverages, and dairy. Calculate the operational friction each one imposes on store teams: receiving dock bottlenecks, invoice verification labor, unauthorized backroom staging, and floor congestion during peak shopping hours. Set strict delivery appointment windows and automated electronic data interchange invoicing. If a vendor generates excessive receiving labor or inconsistent service levels, reduce their allocated shelf footage or move the category to warehouse self-distribution.

3. Rebalance store labor from center-store restocking to perimeter fresh: Use the labor hours freed by SKU reduction to strengthen store execution where regional grocers can set themselves apart: meat, seafood, bakery, and prepared foods. The perimeter of the supermarket is where customer loyalty and trip frequency are built. When center-store SKU counts come down, associates spend fewer hours replenishing dry grocery shelves and managing backroom inventory. Move those hours into fresh food preparation, visual merchandising, and customer service counters. Quality and presentation in perimeter fresh categories help defend the business against hard discounters and online competitors.

4. Make GMROI the executive scorecard for category merchants: Stop using gross margin percentage as the primary scorecard. Category managers frequently protect slow-turning products because they carry a high nominal gross margin percentage, even when the product turns only once every four months, for example. Replace gross margin percentage with Gross Margin Return on Inventory Investment (GMROI), which multiplies gross margin percentage by inventory turnover rate. Review GMROI monthly across every department in executive leadership meetings. Reward merchants who generate rapid inventory turns on essential lines, and hold accountable those who tie up capital in stagnant inventory, regardless of nominal markup percentages.
