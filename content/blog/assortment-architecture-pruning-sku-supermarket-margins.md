# THE SIGNAL

The economic model of regional grocery retail is facing structural compression. For decades, supermarket executives operated under a foundational assumption: shelf variety drives customer loyalty. Store footprints expanded, center-store aisles lengthened, and category managers added thousands of incremental product variations. Every consumer packaged goods brand promised that a new flavor, package size, or seasonal formulation would capture incremental market share.

Authoritative global industry analyses demonstrate that this strategy has reached exhaustion. The comprehensive 2026 State of Grocery North America report published by McKinsey & Company reveals that while total grocery sales grew 1.2 percent in 2025, that expansion was entirely price-driven; unit sales volumes actually contracted by 1.0 percent. Shoppers purchased fewer total physical items. Furthermore, McKinsey discovered that the grocery sector delivered a 12.2 percent weighted average total shareholder return, lagging significantly behind the broader S&P 500 at 18.3 percent. Crucially, 71 percent of consumers now explicitly prefer simpler, lower everyday prices over complex promotional discounting schemes and overwhelming shelf choices. Customers are actively splitting shopping trips across discount, club, and specialty formats to escape category clutter.

The operational penalty of unmanaged product expansion is documented in benchmark research conducted by Boston Consulting Group. Their retail analysis reveals that regional United States grocery chains carry an average of 50 percent more stock-keeping units (SKUs) per linear foot of shelf space than mass and value competitors such as warehouse clubs and hard discounters. This product sprawl drives up supply chain carrying costs, clogs distribution centers, and consumes store labor. However, when grocers execute disciplined offer simplification by pruning 20 percent to 25 percent of SKUs across target categories, Boston Consulting Group proved that sales across those categories actually increased by 2 percent. Removing duplicate, slow-moving items reduced out-of-stock events for core high-velocity products, accelerated truck loading in distribution centers, and drastically reduced store restocking labor hours.

The bottom-line differentiation between average operators and market leaders is definitive. A landmark study of more than 14,000 grocery consumers conducted by Bain & Company reveals that top-performing regional grocery operators sustain operating margins in the 4 percent to 6 percent range, while typical regional grocers struggle in the razor-thin 1 percent to 2 percent band. Bain demonstrated that these high-performing regional leaders outperform peers by enforcing strict cost and category discipline: building high-conviction assortments, driving private label penetration past 30 percent of total sales, and eliminating the operational complexity that erodes supermarket operating margins.

The strategic signal is absolute. In an inflationary, volume-contracting retail environment, attempting to be everything to everyone is financial suicide. Supermarket operating margins are won or lost at the shelf edge. Regional grocers that fail to prune category bloat will see their profitability consumed by inventory carrying costs, store replenishment labor, and chronic supply chain friction.

# THE LENS

This strategic challenge is exceptionally intense across West Michigan. The regional market encompassing Grand Rapids, Holland, Muskegon, Zeeland, Wyoming, and Kalamazoo represents one of the most sophisticated and fiercely contested grocery corridors in the United States. West Michigan is not merely a retail territory; it is the birthplace and corporate headquarters of major grocery powerhouses.

In Walker, Meijer operates as a multi-billion-dollar pioneer of the retail supercenter model, exerting immense regional gravity through massive scale, aggressive general merchandise cross-subsidies, and extensive private brand manufacturing. In Byron Center, SpartanNash anchors wholesale distribution for hundreds of independent grocers across the Midwest while directly operating major retail banners including Family Fare, D&W Fresh Market, Forest Hills Foods, and VG's. Simultaneously, national hard discounters like Aldi have saturated regional strip centers, Costco and Sam's Club dominate suburban bulk fulfillment, and Trader Joe's captures high-income discretionary spending.

In this hyper-competitive environment, regional supermarket operators and multi-store independent grocers face acute structural vulnerability. Unlike national discount giants, regional grocers cannot win a pure price war on commodity dry goods. Unlike specialty gourmet boutiques, they must maintain full-basket shopping credibility for local families.

Caught between these competitive forces, West Michigan supermarket leadership teams repeatedly fall into the Variety Trap. In an effort to defend market share against Meijer and Walmart, regional category managers surrender shelf space to vendor-driven product proliferation. A single commercial aisle in a 55,000-square-foot store displays forty-two variations of olive oil, eighteen distinct package sizes of the same cereal brand, and dozens of slow-turning national brand line extensions.

The consequence is immediate operational drag throughout the regional enterprise. In distribution centers, slow-turning items tie up working capital and pallet positions, forcing warehouse crews into complex, multi-touch picking routines. In retail stores, inventory velocity collapses. A regional store carrying 45,000 distinct SKUs requires store clerks to spend sixty percent of their shifts sorting through backroom overstock, changing paper shelf tags, and rotating dated inventory.

Because shelf space is fragmented across too many secondary items, the store's top twenty percent of revenue-generating SKUs, the essential milk, eggs, bread, and core national brands, are allotted insufficient facings. High-velocity items repeatedly sell out during evening peak hours, creating visible out-of-stocks that alienate shoppers and depress basket totals. Meanwhile, slow-moving specialty items sit on bottom shelves collecting dust until they expire, driving perishable shrink and inventory write-downs.

In an industry where net profit margins hover at two percent, carrying uninspected SKU bloat is an existential threat. West Michigan supermarket executives cannot afford to subsidize vendor line extensions that dilute customer clarity and drain store labor. Corporate strategy must intervene to restore disciplined assortment architecture and protect supermarket operating margins.

# THE DEEPER CONCEPT

This retail transformation is governed by one of Dipriva's foundational philosophies: Discipline of Subtraction.

The prevailing corporate instinct in modern business is additive. When revenue stalls, leadership adds new initiatives. When customer traffic slows, merchandisers add new products. When operations stumble, management adds new reporting tiers. Business culture celebrates addition because adding feels like proactive progress, expansion, and commercial ambition.

In contrast, the Discipline of Subtraction recognizes that complexity is the primary destroyer of operational efficiency and profit margins. Subtraction is not an act of retreat; it is an act of strategic concentration. It is the deliberate, rigorous elimination of low-margin volume, unproductive assets, and operational noise in order to focus capital, talent, and physical infrastructure on the essential core that generates enterprise value.

In supermarket operations, the physics of retail shelf space make the Discipline of Subtraction imperative. A physical grocery store possesses finite physical boundaries: finite linear shelf feet, finite backroom storage cubic footage, and finite employee labor hours. Every slow-turning, low-margin SKU placed on a shelf is not a harmless passive choice; it actively displaces a facing of a high-velocity, high-margin item. It consumes working capital, requires stocking labor, increases scanning friction at checkout, and confuses customer decision-making.

Applying the Discipline of Subtraction to grocery retail requires confronting the Myth of Assortment Completeness. Merchandisers frequently argue that a supermarket must carry five brands of canned water chestnuts or nine package variations of yellow mustard because a customer might walk out if their exact niche preference is missing.

Empirical consumer research dismantles this assumption. When customers confront an overcrowded shelf with dozens of indistinguishable choices, they experience cognitive fatigue. Decision latency increases, purchase hesitation sets in, and customer satisfaction drops. Conversely, when a retailer curates the category down to a dominant national brand, a premium local alternative, and a high-margin private label offering, customer clarity surges. The shopping experience accelerates, shelf availability improves, and overall category sales rise.

The Discipline of Subtraction requires executive leadership to enforce three non-negotiable operational boundaries:
1. Economic Justification: Every SKU must justify its physical footprint through measurable gross margin return on inventory investment, factoring in handling labor and space carry costs.
2. Direct Cannibalization Elimination: Identical product attributes across competing brands must be consolidated into a clear good-better-best architecture.
3. Radical Simplification: Shelf space must be allocated in direct proportion to real consumer velocity, not manufacturer promotional lobbying.

When an executive team masters the Discipline of Subtraction, it stops mistaking activity for progress. Pruning thirty percent of unproductive SKUs liberates warehouse space, slashes store replenishment payroll, eliminates out-of-stocks on vital staples, and directly expands operating cash flow.

# THE DEEPER TIP

Execute a 20-Percent Bottom-SKU Quarantine in your single highest-shrink center-store category this week. You do not need expensive assortment optimization software, third-party syndicated data contracts, or corporate offsite meetings to execute this diagnostic.

Take the following concrete operational action within your retail network:

Select one center-store category that suffers from chronic inventory bloat, high out-of-stocks on top items, or frequent shrink write-downs: breakfast cereals, snack bars, salad dressings, or canned condiments. Pull the trailing twelve-month unit sales, gross margin dollars, and inventory turn rates for every active SKU across that category in three representative stores.

Rank every SKU by total gross margin dollar contribution. Identify the bottom twenty percent of SKUs, the long tail of items that generate negligible cash flow but occupy permanent shelf facings.

Implement an immediate 30-day operational quarantine:
1. Freeze all automated reorders for the bottom twenty percent of SKUs immediately.
2. Consolidate remaining inventory of those items into a single clearance endcap or discount bin to liquidate the stock.
3. Reallocate the newly liberated shelf space immediately to your top ten percent of high-velocity revenue drivers in that category, doubling their shelf facings.
4. Establish a strict zero-out-of-stock mandate on those high-velocity items, ensuring backroom safety stock is aligned with peak weekend demand.

Track total category revenue, gross margin dollars, and store stocking labor hours over the subsequent thirty days.

In almost every grocery pilot, total category revenue will remain stable or increase, out-of-stock incidents on core items will drop to near zero, and store replenishment labor will decline by fifteen percent. You will hold empirical proof that your business does not need catalog sprawl to satisfy customers. You will have demonstrated that category subtraction directly expands operating profitability.

# DIPRIVA ANGLE

What most mid-market grocery executives get wrong about assortment architecture is treating shelf space as real estate to be rented to packaged goods manufacturers rather than strategic infrastructure to be optimized for customer throughput. Category managers routinely accept manufacturer promotional funding, advertising co-ops, and slotting allowances to feature slow-moving product variations, believing they are securing free income for the enterprise. This is financial myopia. The short-term promotional check is completely wiped out by the compounding operational costs of ordering, receiving, warehousing, stocking, and eventually discounting unsold merchandise.

A supermarket is a high-velocity fulfillment pipeline, not a warehouse showroom for vendor experiments. When your corporate strategy permits consumer packaged goods brands to dictate shelf variety, you are outsourcing your operational cost structure to external entities whose financial incentives are directly opposed to your profitability. The brand manufacturer wants maximum shelf facings to block competitors; the supermarket requires maximum inventory turnover per square foot to survive. High-performing grocery executives break this pattern by reclaiming absolute control over assortment architecture, eliminating vendor-driven complexity, and engineering a lean product catalog that maximizes return on invested capital.

# DIPRIVA TIPS

West Michigan supermarket owners, retail executives, and category leaders can eliminate assortment friction and defend operating margins immediately by executing four disciplined strategic interventions:

1. Mandate a One-In, Two-Out Policy for All New Center-Store SKUs.
Halt the unchecked accumulation of product inventory by establishing a non-negotiable catalog gatekeeping rule. Before a category manager or merchant is permitted to introduce a single new manufacturer SKU into store planograms, they must identify and permanently delist two existing SKUs within the same subcategory. The two delisted items must represent lower historical sales velocity and gross margin contribution than the category average. This operational rule forces category managers to evaluate true trade-offs, prevents catalog creep, and continuously cleanses store shelves of dead inventory.

2. Audit Total-Cost-to-Serve Across All Direct-Store-Delivery (DSD) Vendors.
Conduct an immediate financial and operational review of all direct-store-delivery vendors servicing your store network: commercial bread, salty snacks, carbonated beverages, and dairy. Calculate the true operational friction imposed on store teams: receiving dock bottlenecks, invoice verification labor, unauthorized backroom staging, and floor congestion during peak shopping hours. Mandate strict delivery appointment windows and automated electronic data interchange invoicing. If a vendor generates excessive receiving labor or inconsistent service levels, reduce their allocated shelf footage immediately or transition the category to warehouse self-distribution.

3. Rebalance Store Labor from Center-Store Restocking to Perimeter Fresh.
Use the labor hours liberated through SKU reduction to strengthen store-level execution where regional grocers possess a genuine competitive moat: meat, seafood, bakery, and prepared foods. The perimeter of the supermarket is where customer loyalty and trip frequency are established. When center-store SKU counts are trimmed by twenty percent, store associates spend significantly fewer hours replenishing dry grocery shelves and managing backroom inventory. Reallocate those labor hours directly into fresh food preparation, visual merchandising, and customer service counters. Delivering exceptional quality and presentation in perimeter fresh categories directly defends the business against hard discounters and online competitors.

4. Establish an Executive Margin Return on Inventory Metric (GMROI).
Abolish gross margin percentage as the primary performance scorecard for category merchants. Category managers frequently protect slow-turning products because they carry a high nominal gross margin percentage, ignoring that the product turns once every four months. Replace gross margin percentage with Gross Margin Return on Investment (GMROI), which multiplies gross margin percentage by inventory turnover rate. Review GMROI monthly across every department in executive leadership meetings. Reward merchants who generate rapid inventory turns on essential lines, and penalize those who tie up enterprise capital in stagnant inventory, regardless of nominal markup percentages.
