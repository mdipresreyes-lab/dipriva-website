## The Signal

Oracle's capital expenditure reached $55.7 billion in fiscal 2026, up from $21.2 billion the prior year. The company borrowed $43 billion to fund that expansion. Then it announced another round of layoffs.

This is not a story about Oracle being reckless. Oracle's leadership made a calculated bet that AI infrastructure investment is the defining capital decision of this decade, and they committed to it at scale. The layoffs are the consequence of that commitment playing out in real time: when capital flows aggressively toward one category, it has to come from somewhere.

The same week Oracle's headcount decisions made headlines, Target announced it had hired its first Chief AI Officer. Not a VP of Technology. Not a Director of Digital Transformation. A C-Suite executive with board visibility, budget authority, and a mandate to govern AI spending with the same discipline applied to headcount, real estate, and every other major cost center.

Zayo's incoming CEO, Sowmyanarayan Sampath, formerly led Verizon Consumer, one of the most operationally complex consumer businesses in the United States. Zayo did not recruit a technologist. They recruited someone who has managed large-scale operational complexity and knows how to build infrastructure that performs under demand pressure. His mandate, effective September 1, 2026, is to lead Zayo's AI-driven growth phase.

Three signals. One week. A single underlying pattern: the organizations that are navigating AI investment well are treating it as a strategic allocation decision with explicit trade-offs, clear governance, and executive-level accountability. The ones that are not are treating AI as a budget line that grows because no one has built the structure to constrain it.

## The Lens

For a founder or operator, the Oracle story creates a specific kind of pressure. Not the pressure to spend $55 billion on infrastructure. The pressure to answer a question you have probably been avoiding: what is your AI investment actually replacing, and what is it costing you to find out?

Most founders are not making bets at Oracle's scale. But they are making the same category of decision: allocating capital toward AI capabilities before the governance structure exists to evaluate whether that capital is generating a return. The tools are cheaper. The commitments are smaller. The logic trap is identical.

Here is what the Oracle situation actually illustrates. When you increase capital expenditure by 163 percent in a single fiscal year and fund the gap with debt, you are not making one decision. You are making a cascade of downstream decisions that will be made for you by the balance sheet. Headcount becomes a variable cost when fixed costs expand that fast. The layoffs are not a separate event from the investment. They are the same decision, expressed later.

Target's Chief AI Officer hire tells a different story. Target did not announce a major AI spending initiative. They announced a governance structure first. They created the executive function before expanding the budget. That sequencing matters. When a company installs formal oversight before deploying capital, it is signaling that AI is now a discipline, not a department project.

For a founder managing a team of thirty, fifty, or two hundred people, the equivalent of Target's Chief AI Officer hire is not a title. It is a process. It is the moment you decide who owns AI investment decisions, what success looks like at ninety days, and what the explicit exit criteria are if the tool or vendor does not perform. Most companies have not had that conversation. They have subscriptions. They do not have a framework.

Zayo's leadership hire completes the picture. The skills required to manage AI-driven growth are not primarily technical. They are operational. The ability to maintain execution discipline while infrastructure scales rapidly, to make resource allocation decisions under uncertainty, and to ensure the organization's structure is capable of absorbing the growth it is pursuing. That is the constraint most founders will hit before they hit a market constraint.

## The Interweave

The strategic decision making for business owners that actually determines AI outcomes is not the technology selection. It is the capital governance question: what are you willing to stop funding so that this investment has room to prove itself?

That question requires someone in the room who can evaluate the trade-off objectively, structure the governance to enforce it, and identify the internal friction that will slow execution before the initiative even launches. It is the same work that precedes any high-stakes resource allocation decision, whether the context is market entry, a product line, or a leadership restructure. The asset class is new. The analytical discipline is not.

## The Dipriva Concept

### The Discipline of Subtraction

The most expensive thing a business does when it adopts a new capability is fail to subtract anything to make room for it.

Oracle's situation is a magnified version of a decision pattern that plays out at every scale. An organization identifies a strategic priority, allocates capital toward it, and continues funding everything that came before it. The new initiative does not replace existing cost structures. It sits on top of them. The balance sheet eventually corrects the misalignment that leadership avoided addressing directly. In Oracle's case, that correction is headcount. In a smaller company, it is slower to surface and harder to trace back to the original decision.

The Discipline of Subtraction begins with a premise that most growth-oriented founders resist: adding a capability without removing a constraint is not a strategy. It is accumulation. And accumulated cost structures, whether measured in dollars, time, management attention, or organizational complexity, compound against you exactly as efficiently as compounding returns compound for you.

Target's decision to install a Chief AI Officer before expanding AI spending is a form of subtraction. They created a function specifically designed to evaluate what not to fund. The governance structure is the pruning mechanism. Most companies skip this step entirely. They invest in tools, measure activity rather than outcomes, and eventually find themselves running fifteen AI subscriptions with three people who actually use them and no clear owner for any of it.

The founder who applies the Discipline of Subtraction to AI investment asks a different set of questions before the purchase order is signed. What is this replacing? What will we stop doing, stop funding, or stop managing if this works? What is our decision point if it does not? What would we need to see at sixty days to conclude this was the wrong allocation?

These are not questions that slow down decision making. They are questions that eliminate the organizational habit of making irreversible resource commitments as though they are reversible. Oracle borrowed $43 billion on the thesis that AI infrastructure investment is the right allocation for this moment in the market. That may prove to be correct. But the thesis was executed without a visible subtraction discipline, and the workforce is now absorbing the rebalancing.

The discipline is not pessimism about investment. It is precision about trade-offs. Every dollar allocated to AI is a dollar not allocated to something else. Every hour your team spends implementing a new tool is an hour not spent on the work the tool was supposed to accelerate. The executives who are navigating this well, the ones building governance structures before budgets and recruiting for operational complexity rather than technical novelty, are doing so because they have internalized that growth without subtraction is just accumulation wearing a strategy's name.

## The Dipriva Tip

### Audit Your AI Portfolio Against a Single Standard: Replacement, Not Addition

Before approving the next AI tool, subscription, or initiative, run a one-hour internal audit on everything your organization is currently funding in this category. The goal is not to cancel everything. The goal is to establish whether each investment is replacing something or sitting on top of something.

Pull a complete list of every AI-related subscription, tool, and vendor relationship your company currently pays for. Include anything that touches automation, AI writing, AI analytics, AI customer service, or AI productivity. Add the annualized cost for each line item. Then, for each item, answer two questions in writing: What specific process or cost did this replace when we adopted it? Who owns the outcome this tool is supposed to produce?

If you cannot answer both questions for a given line item, that is your signal. Not necessarily a cancellation decision, but a governance gap that requires resolution before you spend another renewal cycle. No AI investment should exist in your budget without a named owner and a documented replacement claim.

Do this exercise with your leadership team present, not your IT team. The reason Target's Chief AI Officer hire is significant is not the title. It is the signal that AI governance has been elevated to the level where strategic trade-offs are made. Your version of that elevation is this conversation, in this room, at this level.

If you execute this correctly, you will find two to three investments that have no clear owner and no documented replacement logic. Consolidate or eliminate them. Redirect that capital toward the one or two tools with measurable output. What you should expect to see within thirty days is not a cost reduction, though that may follow. What you should see is clarity about what your AI investment is actually doing, which is the prerequisite for any decision about what it should do next.
