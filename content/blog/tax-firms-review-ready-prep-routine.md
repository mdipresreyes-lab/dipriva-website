## Black Ore brought big-firm prep to small firms

On October 27, 2026, Black Ore launched Black Ore for Small Firms at Intuit Connect in Las Vegas. It is a version of its Tax Autopilot platform built for small and mid-sized accounting practices. Black Ore states that the largest firms in the country have run that autonomous preparation in production for the last three tax seasons. The small-firm version brings the same preparation workflow to practices that did not have enterprise automation before.

Tax Autopilot takes in source documents in any format, prepares the return, and delivers it review-ready, with every figure linked to its source and items needing attention flagged. That sentence carries the whole product claim, and it also carries the whole management question. A return that arrives review-ready still needs a reviewer, a flag queue, and a sign-off decision. The tool changes who does the first draft. It does not change who signs.

Black Ore for Small Firms is also available natively for Intuit ProConnect Tax and Intuit Lacerte users. Tax Autopilot delivers completed returns directly into those systems, so firms keep the software they already run. Firms using ProConnect Tax receive hands-on migration support from both Intuit and Black Ore. That integration point matters, because a prep tool that lives outside the tax system creates a second place to check, a second export to reconcile, and a second source of truth. A tool that delivers into ProConnect Tax or Lacerte keeps the return in the system the reviewer already trusts.

## Busy season breaks on prep, not demand

Most tax firms do not lose busy season to a lack of clients. They lose it to prep work that arrives late, incomplete, and in no fixed order. A client sends a partial set of documents, then a second set, then a corrected set. A preparer rekeys figures from a scan. A reviewer opens a return and finds no note about which document supports which figure. The return goes back for a missing form. The queue grows, the reviewer becomes the bottleneck, and the firm answers the capacity question with headcount, overtime, or a hard stop on new clients.

That pattern is a workflow failure, not a talent failure. The firm has the expertise to review and sign. It lacks a fixed intake order, a fixed naming rule for source documents, a fixed chase rule for missing items, and a fixed review queue with one owner. Without those four pieces, every return becomes a custom job, and custom jobs do not scale in a fixed season.

Automation that only speeds up drafting does not fix that pattern. It produces more drafts, faster, for the same broken queue. The drafts pile up in front of the same reviewer, with the same missing documents and the same unclear flags. Capacity grows only when the intake, the flag queue, and the sign-off step grow with the drafting speed. That is the routine this post builds.

## Review-ready means traceable, flagged, and signed

Review-ready is a precise claim, and a firm should hold any tool to it in three parts.

First, traceable. Every figure on the return links back to a source document the reviewer can open. If a figure cannot be traced, the reviewer cannot check it, and the return is not review-ready. It is a draft that looks finished. The trace is the check, not a nice extra.

Second, flagged. Items needing attention are marked before the reviewer starts, not discovered by the reviewer during a full rework. A flag queue needs a rule for what counts as a flag, who clears each flag type, and what happens when a flag cannot be cleared from the documents on file. Without that rule, flags become noise, and reviewers learn to ignore them.

Third, signed. A firm professional remains the final authority on sign-off. That authority is not a formality. It is the control that keeps preparation, judgment, and liability in the right place. A return leaves the firm only after a named person has checked the flagged items, checked the traced figures that matter, and signed. No automation step, no queue status, and no deadline pressure replaces that signature.

As an illustration, not a client result and not a statistic: a tax firm receives a client set in three batches, names each file by client and document type, chases the one missing form on a fixed date, and sends the reviewer a return with traced figures and a short flag list. The reviewer clears the flags, signs, and the return moves out. The illustration is useful because it shows the routine, not because it promises a result. The result comes from repetition, and repetition comes from a written routine with an owner.

## The sign-off gate is the control

The sign-off gate is the step that makes automation safe to use at volume. It is a single, written rule: no return leaves the firm without a named reviewer sign-off, and no sign-off happens with an open flag that has no owner and no next action.

That gate does three jobs. It protects the client, because a traced and flagged return gets a human check before it becomes a filing. It protects the firm, because the reviewer who signs is the person who checked the work, not the person who happened to be free. It protects the capacity plan, because the gate makes the real bottleneck visible. If returns stack up at sign-off, the firm has a review capacity problem, not a drafting problem, and it can fix review capacity with a second reviewer, a clearer flag rule, or a smaller intake batch.

The gate also sets a boundary for automation. Automation can ingest documents, map them to forms, populate the tax system, and draft the summary. Automation does not approve a return, change a source record, or send a filing to a third party without a human approval step. Owners keep the final say on financial, client-facing, and public changes. That boundary is not a lack of trust in the tool. It is the control that makes the tool safe to use every week of busy season.

Firms that skip the gate pay for it in rework. A return that leaves with an open flag returns as a correction, a client question, or an amended filing. Each of those costs more time than the sign-off check would have cost, and each one breaks the trust the firm sells. The gate is slow on purpose, and that slowness is the point.

## Per-return pricing changes the staffing math

Pricing is per return, with no enterprise contract commitments and no five-figure minimum. That pricing sentence changes the buying decision for a small or mid-sized firm. The firm does not need to sign an enterprise deal, pay a large minimum, or commit to a volume tier before it knows whether the routine works. It can test prep capacity return by return, with its own clients, its own documents, and its own reviewers.

That change does not remove the price question. The October 27 announcement states per-return pricing, and it does not list a dollar price per return. A firm should check the current per-return terms before it buys, and it should price that cost against its own fee per return, its own review time per return, and its own capacity target for the season. A tool that costs less per return and still leaves the review queue broken saves drafting time and keeps the bottleneck.

Per-return pricing also changes the staffing question in a useful way. Instead of asking whether to hire for busy season, the firm can ask a sharper question: how many returns can one reviewer sign per week, with traced figures and a cleared flag queue, and how many returns does the firm need to sign to meet its season target. That question has an answer the firm can plan around. Headcount is one answer to a capacity gap. A fixed intake order, a fixed flag rule, and a fixed sign-off gate are other answers, and they cost less than a hire that leaves after the season.

## Seven steps that make prep review-ready

1. Audit intake and list only the document types, channels, and tax system the firm will use each week. Close or leave out the rest, so maintenance stays small.
2. Set one naming rule for source documents and one intake checklist per return type. Fix the checklist before the season starts, so every preparer and every client uses the same order.
3. Set a missing-document chase with a fixed date and a fixed owner. A return does not enter the review queue until its documents are complete, or until the missing item has a named next action.
4. Send the reviewer a traced return with a short flag list. Write the flag rule in advance: what counts as a flag, who clears each flag type, and what happens when a flag cannot be cleared.
5. Run one review queue with one owner per return. The reviewer clears flags, checks traced figures, and signs, in that order, with no return skipping the queue.
6. Enforce the sign-off gate on every return. No signature with an open flag that has no owner, and no filing without a signature.
7. Write the routine down, train the team, and review it after the first busy-season batch. Correct the routine where returns stall, then hand it to the named owners with dates.

That list is the whole routine in miniature. It does not require a new department, a new hire, or a new stack of tools. It requires clean inputs, a traced return, a flag rule, a sign-off gate, and a written routine. Firms that complete those seven steps have a prep routine they can run.
