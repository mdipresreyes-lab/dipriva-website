# THE SIGNAL

Running a 10 to 50 person service business has become significantly more expensive over the past twenty-four months, yet most owners keep their billing rates flat. Experienced accountants, insurance producers, roofing foremen, and software technicians command higher compensation. Specialized software subscriptions, liability coverage, and commercial office leases continue to climb.

The data proves that this squeeze is nationwide across Main Street employer firms. According to the 2026 Report on Employer Firms from the Federal Reserve Banks Small Business Credit Survey, 77 percent of small employer firms reported that the rising costs of goods, services, and wages represented a top operational and financial challenge. The survey revealed that small businesses are now more likely to report that revenues decreased rather than increased over the prior twelve months, with future revenue expectations falling to their lowest levels since 2020.

At the same time, disciplined operators have already begun correcting their rates. The National Federation of Independent Business reported in its Small Business Economic Trends survey that a net 38 percent of small business owners raised their average selling prices, while a net 32 percent plan further price increases over the coming quarter. Furthermore, the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics reported in its Employment Cost Index that compensation costs for private industry workers increased 3.3 percent and benefit costs rose 3.8 percent for the 12-month period ending in June 2026.

If your operational delivery costs climbed this year and your service pricing remained frozen, you did not protect your client base. You absorbed an unforced pay cut and transferred firm equity directly to your accounts.

# THE LENS

This margin compression hits owners of 10 to 50 person service firms directly. If you lead a bookkeeping practice, an independent insurance agency, a roofing outfit, or an IT managed service provider, you are not insulated by corporate layers. You still sell the major accounts, manage key client relationships, and step in when technical delivery hits an obstacle.

When margins shrink, you cannot trim administrative fat that does not exist. You personally absorb the deficit by working longer hours, reviewing tax returns, writing proposals, or auditing installations because you cannot afford senior staff at market rates. You feel trapped between two bad choices: leave prices where they are and watch profits erode, or raise rates and risk alienating clients who helped build the firm.

This fear is almost always exaggerated. In a 20-person firm, the owner assumes that long-standing accounts stay because of low prices. That is rarely true. Your best accounts stay because you answer the phone, solve difficult problems without drama, and deliver predictable outcomes. When an owner hesitates to implement a structured service business pricing strategy, the firm takes on low-margin volume to make payroll, which overburdens staff, degrades delivery, and frustrates top-tier accounts.

Your best accounts value dependability far more than cheap rates. The real danger is not that raising rates will prompt top clients to leave; the danger is that keeping rates artificially low will degrade your ability to deliver the level of service they depend on.

# THE DEEPER CONCEPT

The barrier that prevents service business owners from adjusting rates is rooted in a failure of Assumption Testing.

Assumption Testing is the disciplined practice of identifying unproven executive beliefs, treating them as falsifiable hypotheses, and gathering objective empirical evidence before committing resources or avoiding necessary decisions.

In a 10 to 50 person service business, the owner operates under three unexamined assumptions:
1. "My best clients will leave if I raise my fees."
2. "Clients buy our services primarily because we are affordable."
3. "A price increase requires an exhaustive justification of our internal operational costs."

Every one of these assumptions is demonstrably false.

First, your best clients run busy organizations. They know wages and software costs have increased. What they fear is not a reasonable price adjustment; what they fear is operational disruption, missed deadlines, and poor communication. If an accounting firm charges $1,500 a month for payroll and bookkeeping, a bump to $1,800 is an immaterial line item for a thriving business client. Finding and onboarding a new firm would cost that client tens of thousands of dollars in lost management time.

Second, top-tier accounts do not buy on price. They buy on certainty and peace of mind, knowing that when an emergency arises, your team will resolve it correctly. When you fail to test the assumption that clients are price-sensitive, you project your internal anxieties onto your client base.

Third, clients do not care about your overhead expenses. Telling a client you are raising rates because your health insurance premiums increased sounds apologetic. Professional pricing reflects the value delivered, the priority access provided, and the commercial risk removed from the client plate.

When you apply Assumption Testing, price adjustments cease to be a dangerous gamble. You update pricing on upcoming new proposals first, observe the close rate, and test a rate adjustment on a small cohort of existing clients. When data reveals zero defections, fear evaporates, and you roll out adjustments across remaining accounts with confidence.

# THE DEEPER TIP

Test your true pricing ceiling this week by executing the New Proposal Markup Test on your next three prospective clients.

You do not need to announce a firm-wide rate change to understand how to raise prices in a service business. Isolate your next three sales opportunities.

Take three concrete steps:
1. Prepare your standard scope of work and pricing calculation as you normally would for these three prospects.
2. Apply an immediate, flat 20 percent markup across the entire proposal. If your standard fee for a commercial roofing repair or annual tax engagement was $10,000, price the proposal at $12,000.
3. Present the proposal with absolute conviction. Do not offer unprompted discounts, do not apologize for the number, and do not explain your internal margin calculations. Focus the sales conversation entirely on client timeline, scope of risk, and certainty of execution.

Track the outcome of those three proposals. In the vast majority of service firms, at least two of the three prospects will accept the higher rate without hesitation. Closing even one new account at a 20 percent premium immediately resets your psychological baseline. It provides empirical proof that your market readily supports higher rates, giving you objective confidence to address legacy accounts.

# DIPRIVA ANGLE

What most service business owners get wrong about raising prices on existing clients is treating it as an awkward personal confrontation rather than a standard commercial realignment. Because the owner built relationships through personal charm and hands-on service, they feel guilty asking for more money. They wait three or four years without touching rates, allowing inflation to hollow out operating margins. When pressure finally becomes unbearable, they send an emotional, defensive email announcing a sudden across-the-board hike, triggering unnecessary friction.

A mature service business does not apologize for sustaining healthy profit margins. Healthy margins are what allow you to pay top compensation, retain experienced staff, invest in secure infrastructure, and provide responsive client service. When an underpriced firm refuses to adjust rates, it is forced to overwork staff and cut corners on quality. Raising rates with discipline is an act of stewardship that protects both your employees and the long-term integrity of your client relationships.

# DIPRIVA TIPS

Owners of 10 to 50 person service firms can execute a disciplined price increase without losing core accounts by taking three structured actions:

1. Segment Your Client Roster into Value Quartiles.
Never apply an indiscriminate, blanket rate increase across your entire client base. Export your trailing twelve-month revenue by client and calculate the gross margin and partner hours dedicated to each account. Divide your roster into distinct tiers: top accounts (high profit, low friction), steady accounts (reliable profit), and low-margin accounts (high administrative demand). Your best clients deserve personal communication and grandfathered transition periods. Chronic underpaying accounts must receive immediate rate adjustments or be pruned to liberate delivery capacity.

2. Bundle Price Increases with Scope Clarification.
Do not send a rate increase notification that simply demands more money for the exact same undefined bucket of work. Pair your price adjustment with a structured scope reset. Clearly document what is included in your standard service agreement and establish explicit fees for out-of-scope requests, emergency turnaround times, and weekend access. Clients accept price adjustments far more readily when they see that the agreement protects them from unexpected billing surprises while establishing clear operational boundaries.

3. Provide Sixty Days Written Notice and Personal Phone Outreach for Top Accounts.
For your top 20 percent of accounts, never communicate a rate increase through an invoice footnote or generic mass email. Pick up the telephone. Schedule a brief, proactive check-in call with the primary stakeholder. Review the tangible business outcomes delivered over the past year, outline the service enhancements planned for the upcoming year, and state the new rate directly. Give them sixty days of notice before the new billing schedule takes effect, allowing them to adjust internal budgets smoothly.
