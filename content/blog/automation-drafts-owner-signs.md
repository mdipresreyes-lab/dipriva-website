A weekly automation routine is the work that turns two new tools into capacity for a tax firm. This post covers QX TaxReady, launched in the United States on November 3, 2026, and Catalyst, announced by Constant Contact on November 12, 2026. One prepares tax returns at one fixed price per return. The other drafts a marketing plan each week. Both follow the same rule: automation drafts, the owner signs. A tax firm that runs that rule in both places gets its hours back, and those hours go to the work only the firm can do.

## QX TaxReady prepares returns at one fixed price

On November 3, 2026, QX Global Group launched QX TaxReady in the United States. QX TaxReady is a fully managed tax preparation service, powered by AI and delivered by a team of more than 500 dedicated tax specialists. QX AI agents pull data from client documents and prepare the return. Every return then goes through a four-eyed review: a qualified preparer and a senior reviewer each check it and correct anything that needs fixing before it reaches the firm. The firm gets a clean return, ready for its own final review and signature.

That sentence is the whole service, and it answers the question a tax firm should ask first about any prep tool: who checks the work, and who signs it. The answer here has three names. The AI agent prepares. A qualified preparer checks. A senior reviewer checks again. Then the firm signs. No step in that chain is skipped, and the firm keeps the last step.

The price is part of the same answer. Firms send their client documents, QX prepares and reviews each return, and the firm signs off. There is one price per return and no hourly billing. The price stays the same in October and on April 15, in the words of Vijay Pahuja, Group CEO of QX Global Group, quoted in the launch release. A firm that knows its cost per return before the season can set its fee per return, count its returns, and plan its capacity without hiring for the season. That is the fixed price doing real work: it turns capacity from a guess into a number.

The turnaround time is set on the QX TaxReady page for CPA firms. The standard turnaround time is 24 to 48 hours for simple 1040s and 48 to 72 hours for complex 1040 and business returns. A firm should read that as a standard, not a promise for every return, because a return with missing documents still waits on the client. The routine that makes the turnaround real is the same one from the Canopy post last week: clean intake, a fixed chase for missing documents, and a review queue with one owner per return.

Two cautions belong here, stated plainly. First, the final price per return is not published. The QX page says one fixed price per return, with everything included, and asks the firm to submit a form to get its final pricing. This post states no dollar amount for QX TaxReady, because none is published. Second, the release carries vendor performance claims, and this post does not repeat them. The release states a first-pass accuracy figure, a handling time reduction, and a cost reduction against traditional outsourcing. Those are vendor claims, not independently verified figures, so they are left out, following the same call made for the Canopy and Black Ore posts.

## Catalyst drafts the marketing plan each week

On November 12, 2026, Constant Contact announced Catalyst at its GURU Conference. Constant Contact, the marketing platform that helps small businesses grow, announced Constant Contact Catalyst, an AI marketing solution that plans, executes, and drives growth across marketing channels, giving small business owners expert-level work while maintaining control.

Catalyst starts where most small firms start: no marketing staff, and an owner who handles marketing alongside everything else. Catalyst will scan a business digital footprint to understand its voice, then build an itemized plan that an SMB can review in a chat interface, enabling brainstorming and strategy from a single prompt. The plan covers email, social sites like Facebook and Instagram, SMS, ads, Google business profile, and the firm website. Catalyst creates the content, humans approve it, and Catalyst handles the execution on their behalf.

The weekly part is what makes Catalyst a routine instead of a one-time draft. Catalyst turns that information into a customized marketing plan each week, which the customer can review and approve. After a campaign goes out, Catalyst ties results from one week to the next, so the next plan starts from what the last one did. The owner stays in control at every step: the owner chooses which channels Catalyst uses, edits anything in the proposed plan, turns off access to any channel, and approves the plan before it goes live.

One more caution belongs here, and it is bigger than the QX caution. Catalyst is not a product a firm can buy today. Small business owners can join Catalyst's early access program at constantcontact.com/catalyst. No general availability date has been announced, and no price has been published. There is no independent evidence yet of how well it performs for real businesses, because it is at the early access waitlist stage. This post describes what Constant Contact announced, not what any firm has measured. A firm that joins the waitlist should treat it as a pilot, with one named approver and a fixed weekly review, and should judge it on its own results before it becomes part of the routine.

## Automation drafts, the owner approves

The two tools look different, and they are. One prepares tax returns. The other writes marketing. But the pattern is the same, and the pattern is the point.

First, the draft comes from automation. QX AI agents pull data from client documents and prepare the return. Catalyst scans the digital footprint and builds the plan. In both cases the draft arrives fast, in a fixed format, and ready to check.

Second, a human checks the draft before it goes anywhere. Every QX return goes through a four-eyed review, a qualified preparer and a senior reviewer, before it reaches the firm. Every Catalyst plan waits for the owner to review it in a chat interface before Catalyst executes it. In both cases the check is built into the workflow, not added after the fact.

Third, the owner signs. The firm gets a clean return, ready for its own final review and signature. The owner approves the marketing plan before it goes live. In both cases the signature is the control, and the control stays with the firm.

That pattern is why the two tools belong in one post. A firm that buys automation without a sign-off step buys speed and risk together. A firm that buys the pattern, draft, check, sign, buys speed with the risk held. The sign-off is not a delay in the workflow. The sign-off is the workflow.

## The saved hours go to core work

A tax firm that runs this pattern in two places buys back hours in two places, and the hours go to the same place: the work only the firm can do.

On the tax side, the hours come from prep. The AI agent pulls data from client documents and prepares the return, the two reviewers check it, and the firm reviewer signs a clean return instead of keying one. The firm reviewer still reviews, and still signs, because the signature is the firm's responsibility. What changes is what the reviewer spends the review on: judgment about the return, not data entry into it.

On the marketing side, the hours come from drafting. Catalyst builds the itemized plan, creates the content, and handles execution after approval. The owner still reviews the plan each week, and still approves it, because the voice and the offer are the firm's responsibility. What changes is what the owner spends the review on: whether the plan says the right thing to the right people, not whether a post got written at all.

As an illustration, not a client result and not a statistic: a tax firm sends its client documents to QX TaxReady on Monday, reviews the clean returns as they come back through the week, and signs them. On Friday, the owner opens the Catalyst weekly plan, checks the email and the social posts against the firm voice, approves them, and Catalyst publishes. The firm did not hire a preparer for the season, and it did not hire a marketer for the year. It spent its week on client advice, on relationships, and on the final review, which is the work a client pays the firm for. The illustration serves because it shows the routine, not because it promises a result. The result comes from repetition, and repetition comes from a weekly routine with a named approver.

That is the core competency argument, stated without jargon. A tax firm is not a data entry shop, and it is not a content shop. It is a firm that advises clients, keeps their trust, and signs returns it stands behind. Every hour spent keying a document or drafting a post is an hour not spent on that work. Automation that drafts, and a human who signs, moves the hour back to where it earns its keep.

## Two tools, one weekly review routine

The routine that holds both tools together fits on one page, and it has one owner.

First, name the approver. One person approves the tax returns, and one person approves the marketing plan each week. It can be the same person in a small firm. It cannot be nobody.

Second, fix the weekly review. The tax review runs as returns come back, with a queue and one owner per return. The marketing review runs once a week, at a fixed time, with the plan, the drafts, and the results from the week before on the same screen. A review with no fixed time does not happen, and a tool with no review is not a routine.

Third, keep the sign-off visible. The firm signs the return. The owner approves the plan before it goes live. Both sign-offs are recorded, with a date, so the firm can show who approved what and when.

Fourth, judge the tools on the firm's own results. For QX TaxReady, the measures are turnaround time against the standard, returns that come back clean, and cost per return against the fixed price. For Catalyst, the measures are whether the plan goes out every week, whether the owner approves it without rewriting it, and whether the campaigns bring in the calls and bookings the firm wants. Neither tool gets judged on the vendor claims in its launch release. Both get judged on the firm's own numbers, after a pilot, before either becomes part of the routine.

Fifth, keep the routine running after the season. Tax prep peaks in the spring, and marketing runs all year. A routine that stops in April is not a routine. The weekly marketing review keeps running through the summer, when the firm has the hours to use it, and the tax review queue is ready when the next season starts.
